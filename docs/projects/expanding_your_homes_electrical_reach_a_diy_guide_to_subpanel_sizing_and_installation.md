# Expanding Your Home’s Electrical Reach A DIY Guide to Subpanel Sizing and Installation

As homeowners add high-demand appliances—such as EV chargers, workshop tools, or finished basement circuits—they often encounter a crowded main service panel. While the temptation is to keep "tandem" breaking the existing space, the more professional and scalable solution is the installation of a subpanel. 

A subpanel (or secondary distribution point) acts as a satellite to your main service entrance. It doesn’t provide more total power to the home, but it redistributes the existing capacity to a specific area, simplifying cable runs and providing localized overcurrent protection.

## The Core Technical Requirement: The Four-Wire Rule

The most critical distinction between a main service panel and a subpanel is how the neutral and ground wires are handled. In a main panel, the neutral bus and the grounding bus are "bonded" (connected) together. 

In a subpanel, **neutral and ground must remain isolated.** This requires a four-wire feeder:
1.  **Hot Leg 1 (Black)**
2.  **Hot Leg 2 (Red)**
3.  **Neutral (White)**
4.  **Equipment Ground (Green or Bare)**

If you bond the neutral and ground in a subpanel, you create a "parallel path" for return current. This means the metal casing of the panel and any connected conduit could become energized under normal operating conditions—a significant shock hazard.

## Sizing Your Subpanel Feeder

Choosing the right wire gauge for your subpanel depends on the "Load Calculation" of the devices you plan to run and the distance from the main panel. Over longer distances (typically 100+ feet), you must account for **voltage drop** by increasing the wire size.

### Technical Specs: Feeder Wire Sizing (Copper vs. Aluminum)

The following table outlines common subpanel sizes and the required conductor gauges for typical residential runs (under 50 feet).

| Subpanel Amperage | Copper Wire Gauge (THHN/THWN) | Aluminum Wire Gauge (XHHW-2) | Recommended Conduit Size (PVC) | Common Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **30 Amps** | 10 AWG | 8 AWG | 3/4" | Small Shed / Lighting |
| **50 Amps** | 8 AWG | 6 AWG | 1" | EV Charger / Power Tools |
| **60 Amps** | 6 AWG | 4 AWG | 1" | Home Office / Small Guest Room |
| **100 Amps** | 3 AWG | 1 AWG | 1-1/4" | Detached Garage / Workshop |
| **125 Amps** | 1 AWG | 2/0 AWG | 1-1/2" | Full Basement / ADU |

*Note: Always verify with the current NEC (National Electrical Code) or your local jurisdiction, as temperature ratings of lugs and insulation type can shift these requirements.*

## DIY Installation Workflow

### 1. The Feeder Breaker
The subpanel is protected by a double-pole breaker located in the main service panel. This breaker determines the maximum capacity of the subpanel. If you install a 60A breaker in the main panel, your subpanel is a 60A branch, even if the subpanel "lugs" are rated for 125A.

### 2. Conduit and Routing
For indoor DIY projects, Romex (NM-B) is often used if the cable is protected within walls. However, for workshops or garages, running individual THHN/THWN wires through EMT (Electrical Metallic Tubing) or PVC conduit is preferred. Conduit allows for easier upgrades in the future and provides superior physical protection.

### 3. Separation of Bus Bars
When you purchase a "main lug" panel to use as a subpanel, it often comes with a green bonding screw or a bonding strap. **Do not install it.** You must ensure the neutral bus is sitting on its plastic insulator and the ground bus is screwed directly into the metal cabinet of the panel.

### 4. Torque Matters
A common failure point in DIY electrical work is improper terminal tension. Loose connections cause resistance, which leads to heat and potential fires. Use a torque screwdriver or wrench to meet the manufacturer’s specifications (usually printed on the door label or the breaker itself), typically measured in inch-pounds (in-lb).

## When to Call a Pro
While mounting the subpanel and pulling wire is within the reach of a skilled DIYer, the final "hot" connections in the main service panel are dangerous. If your main panel does not have a single main disconnect (meaning the bus bars stay energized even when the individual breakers are off), contact a licensed electrician to perform the final tie-in and to ensure your home's total load calculation remains within the limits of your utility service.
